Full Fathom Five: Transforming the New Variorum
Shakespeare Through Computational Access
Jack Carson
jdcarson@mit.edu
Abstract
This paper introduces a digital system designed to overcome persistent challenges in accessing
the New Variorum Shakespeare (NVS) editions. Our methodology involves comprehensive
corpus digitization and adaptive interface design. The NVS, a repository of over a century
of Shakespearean scholarly endeavor, has historically been challenging to utilize owing to its
limited physical availability, antiquated textual formats, and complex citation methods. Our
solution integrates a multi-phase optical character recognition (OCR) process with validation by
large language models. It pairs the Variorum’s old-spelling apparatus with modernized reading
texts, aligned to the Folger Digital Texts for one play so far, with the remaining plays using
each edition’s own lineation. The developed platform offers line-by-line access to historical
annotations and incorporates experimental artificial intelligence–driven analytical functions.
These functions are grounded in public-domain reference works—C. T. Onions’s A Shakespeare
Glossary, Alexander Schmidt’s Shakespeare-Lexicon, period dictionaries from the University of
Toronto’s Lexicons of Early Modern English (LEME), and the 1599 Geneva Bible—and serve
as supplementary research tools rather than foundational scholarly resources. A multi-part
verification supports the corpus: residual truncation stands at 1.0% by human-adjudicated
sampling, and 98.4% of note endings match independent print witnesses. A stratified full-span
sample found no genuine interior divergence after adjudication of automated failures. This
project advances digital humanities methodologies by illustrating that the infrastructure for
accessing historical scholarly editions is fundamentally different from the process of creating new
critical editions. This distinction carries substantial implications for the application, evaluation,
and comparative analysis of computational methods against TEI-based approaches. The platform
encompasses all twenty-one pre-1956 Variorum dramatic editions in the public domain and is
publicly accessible at https://newvariorum.com.
Keywords — Digital Humanities, Shakespeare Studies, Electronic Scholarly Editions, Computa-
tional Bibliography, Optical Character Recognition, Large Language Models
Introduction
The Democratic Vision and Its Technical Barriers
This paper introduces a new web platform, www.newvariorum.com, that places the historical New
Variorum Shakespeare editions online, with line-level access to their accumulated commentary. The
name for the most capable part of this new platform, called “Full Fathom Five,” and the title
of the accompanying paper is derived from Ariel’s lyrical passage in Shakespeare’s The Tempest
(1.2.396–401), which describes a father lying “full fathom five” and undergoing a “sea-change” into
something “rich and strange.” This metaphor is fitting, as it illustrates how historical scholarship,

often obscured by material constraints and antiquated conventions, can be transformed and made
more accessible without sacrificing its fundamental essence.
In 1871, Horace Howard Furness, a Philadelphia attorney possessed by an eccentric if almost
unrivaled passion for literature, initiated what turned out to be one of the most singular endeavors in
the history of academic research, the New Variorum Shakespeare. The term “variorum” itself finds
its origin in the Latin phrase “editio cum notis variorum,” signifying an edition that incorporates
the annotations of multiple editors and commentators. Furness appended “new” to his own effort
in an attempt to differentiate his project from preceding variorum editions of Shakespeare, the
most recent of which was the Boswell–Malone edition published in 1821. His conception was
as groundbreaking as his ambition was unconstrained; he aimed to consolidate within a single
publication all noteworthy commentary, glosses, interpretations, and textual variations that had
emerged concerning Shakespeare’s works since their initial printing more than two centuries earlier.
This undertaking transcended mere editorial work, embodying a democratic aspiration to make the
entirety of scholarly discourse on Shakespeare, previously confined to scarce volumes in exclusive
libraries, accessible to any earnest reader. Furness articulated this vision in the preface to the
inaugural volume where he stated that his objective was to afford “the humblest student” the “same
advantages as the most learned professor.”
Furness labored indefatigably on the New Variorum for over four decades. Working from his home,
he meticulously compiled the first volume, Romeo and Juliet , in 1871. To do so, Furness amassed
editions and commentaries on the play dating back to Nicholas Rowe’s 1709 edition, recognized as
the first scholarly attempt to edit Shakespeare’s plays. By the time of his death in 1912, Furness had
personally overseen the editorial process for fifteen of Shakespeare’s plays in the variorum format,
including all of the major tragedies. The series was, in the words of one subsequent scholar, “the
ultimate reference edition for Shakespeare’s works, the place where a serious student may find an
expression or at least a record of every significant critical action performed on Shakespeare’s text
up to the edition’s cutoff date” (Turner, 1986, p. 10).
Long before his death, Furness enlisted his son, Horace Howard Furness Jr., to continue the work.
For his part, Furness Jr. edited four additional plays before his death in 1930. In 1933, the New
Variorum Shakespeare became an official project of the Modern Language Association of America
(MLA), which provided institutional infrastructure and editorial oversight through the twentieth
century, publishing volumes including Marvin Spevack’s Antony and Cleopatra (1990) and Richard
Knowles’s two-volume King Lear (2020).
In 2019, the series entered a new phase when Laura Mandell negotiated with the MLA to move the
NVS to Texas A&M University and make it freely accessible online (Mandell, 2019). That project
produces new born-digital critical editions; its relationship to the present work is discussed under
Related Work below.
The language of “democratic access,” at least as Furness was to use it, is best understood as a practical
scholarly aspiration rather than as a claim that every barrier to Shakespearean scholarship can be
eliminated by software alone. Furness’s “humblest student” still needed literacy, scholarly curiosity,
and access to the intellectual conventions of the archive. This platform, www.newvariorum.com,
materially advances that aspiration by removing several of the most concrete obstacles to use—
physical scarcity, difficult typography, and the absence of line-level digital access—and by reducing
the opacity of the citation abbreviations through digitization-time expansion of critic surnames and
per-play critic indexes. In doing so, it makes a historically elite reference tradition substantially
more navigable for students, teachers, and researchers who would otherwise be unlikely to encounter

Related Work
This project integrates three scholarly areas: the digitization of historical editions, scholarly digital
edition theory, and the application of large language models in editorial work.
The digital Shakespeare landscape is extensive, and this platform is meant to complement existing
resources. The Internet Shakespeare Editions (Best, 2008) provide modern, peer-reviewed, open-
access editions with critical apparatus. Open Source Shakespeare (n.d.) offers concordance and
search functionalities across the canon. The New Variorum Shakespeare, now a digital-first project
hosted by Texas A&M University’s Center of Digital Humanities Research (Mandell, 2019), produces
new, born-digital critical editions encoded in TEI, wedding contemporary analysis to a peer-reviewed
apparatus. As of this writing, two editions have been published— The Winter’s Tale and A
Midsummer Night’s Dream —with further editions in production (New Variorum Shakespeare, 2022).
That project’s digital ambitions trace back to the MLA’s own Electronic New Variorum Shakespeare
effort associated with the Knowles King Lear (Werstine, 2008), which produced prototypes but no
public platform before the series moved to Texas A&M. However, that project has not published
digitized versions of the historical Furness volumes.
The instant project differs in both stance and scope from the ongoing New Variorum work. While the
A&M project re-edits individual plays through a slow, exacting process (with only two completed),
this platform offers comprehensive access to twenty-one public-domain pre-1956 editions. It transmits
these faithfully rather than re-editing them. The two projects are complementary, as, for plays
available in both, a reader gains access to both the historical Variorum record and contemporary
editorial work.
The closest precedent to this project for digital access to variorum commentary is Bernice Kliman’s
extraordinary work on Hamlet. Her online resource Enfolded Hamlet presents the play’s competing
early texts within a single navigable apparatus (Kliman, 1996), and her website HamletWorks, which
grew out of her coordination of the New Variorum Hamlet, assembles searchable commentary notes,
textual notes, and digitized editions for the play (Kliman et al., n.d.; Kliman, 2006). Kliman’s
projects demonstrated both the demand for, and the difficulty of, digital variorum access; they
treat a single play in maximum depth. Extending this approach, the present platform provides
line-level access across twenty-one historical editions. Building access infrastructure, rather than a
new critical edition, is a deliberate choice of this project, one that responds to active discussions
surrounding scholarly digital editions. Sahle (2016), for example, distinguishes the digital edition as
a methodological paradigm, not merely text digitization; Pierazzo (2015) thoroughly explains how
editorial models change with the medium. This project does not claim to produce new editorial
judgment; its ambition and purported authority derive from the attempt to faithfully transmit an
existing printed apparatus.
Artificial intelligence is a new tool in the digital humanities, but one that has not shown unalloyed
success. In particular, the use of large language models (LLMs), as in this project, for post-correction
of optical character recognition (OCR) from historical texts has produced rather mixed results.
Boros et al. (2024), evaluating fourteen models across eight historical benchmarks in text-only
settings, found that unconstrained LLM post-correction usually degrades the input, with the worst
failures being paraphrase and outright invention of new text; Zhang et al. (2024), by contrast,
showed that carefully configured GPT-4-class models can meaningfully improve recognition of
challenging period typography. The even more recent literature has complicated rather than

settled the question. Kanerva et al. (2025) found open-weight models a promising intervention
on English historical corpora but falling short of practical utility for Finnish texts; in the end,
they adjudged commercial models cost-infeasible at collection scale. Greif et al. (2025) report
that image-conditioned multimodal post-correction produces large accuracy gains on historical
documents without fine-tuning. Bourne (2025) demonstrates substantial error reductions from
synthetic-noise fine-tuning but at the cost of the kind of curated training infrastructure this project
does not assume. On evaluation, Levchenko (2025) finds that post-OCR LLM correction can
degrade strong multimodal OCR outright and that character error rates miss period-specific failures
such as over-historicization, while Backer and Hyman (2025) argue that standard OCR metrics
underestimate what matters for historical research. Together, these findings shaped this project’s
approach to OCR. Correction for the www.newvariorum.com website is based on the page image
rather than left to free-running generation; no fine-tuned correction model is assumed. The output
is verified against independent print witnesses, with the paraphrase and invention failure modes
that Boros et al. document serving as explicit targets of the fidelity checks reported below, which
found no instance of either. The question this platform answers is accordingly different from the
one the post-correction benchmarks pose, e.g., not whether an LLM improves character error rate
on a held-out sample, but whether a staged OCR-plus-LLM ingest preserves variorum note content
against independent print witnesses at corpus scale.
Worryingly, a considerable body of evidence exists regarding how models tend to generate plausible
yet fictitious citations and unsubstantiated statements. Both Walters and Wilder (2023) and Sun
et al. (2024) have documented this problem. This project’s design architecture addresses the risk
of the model generating fictitious citations or unsubstantiated statements through deterministic
retrieval of verbatim historical text and server-side overwrites of generated commentary with source
apparatus. Its contribution is a demonstration of a conservative design pattern for generative models
whereby fidelity to sources is unyielding.
The Scope and Challenge of the New Variorum Corpus
The complete pre-1956 New Variorum Shakespeare comprises twenty-five editions: twenty-three play
editions and two editions of the non-dramatic poetry. The dramatic editions contain approximately
15,000 pages of accumulated scholarship representing over 1,000 critics, editors, and commentators
from 1709 to 1955. This project focuses exclusively on the dramatic works, as they represent the
core of the Variorum tradition and present the most complex challenges for line-level annotation
alignment.
This scholarly wealth remains largely inaccessible due to barriers embedded in the editorial tradition.
The Variorum maintains original folio and quarto spellings that impede comprehension for modern
readers. References frequently appear as fragments—“Cap.” for Edward Capell, “Steev.” for
George Steevens, “Clar.” for the Clarendon edition of Clark and Wright—requiring consultation
of bibliographic tables located dozens or hundreds of pages from the commentary itself. Many
significant figures are also discussed in prose introductions and appendices rather than in the formal
bibliographic apparatus, which makes computational identification especially valuable. This is not
to forget that complete sets of the New Variorum Shakespeare are scarce, and its volumes are often
exiled to research libraries or special collections.

Methodology and System Development
Corpus Acquisition
Digital surrogates were sourced from Internet Archive and HathiTrust for the twenty-one pre-1956
NVS dramatic editions that are in the public domain. The two remaining pre-1956 editions— Troilus
and Cressida (1953) and Richard II (1955), both late products of the original MLA-era series before
the project’s move to Texas A&M—remain under copyright and are excluded from the platform.
All volumes in the corpus are in the public domain in the United States: the Furness-era volumes
by age, and the later volumes (Hemingway 1936, Shaaber 1940) on the basis of renewal searches in
the Stanford Copyright Renewal Database and the United States Copyright Office records, which
found no renewal registrations; under pre-1978 United States law, publication without subsequent
renewal places a work in the public domain. The Folger Digital Texts, used for line alignment, are
licensed under Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial terms (CC BY-NC 3.0).
The scans of the New Variorum print editions yielded page-level scans at 300–400 dots per inch
(DPI). This is sufficient for optical character recognition. Each volume was triaged by scan quality,
particularly character-level clarity in footnote regions, preservation of marginal line numbers, and
the presence of bleed-through or foxing. Fuzzier volumes, notably the 1877 Hamlet and the 1907
Antony and Cleopatra, received additional manual correction passes. In cases where both the Internet
Archive and HathiTrust had copies of the same book, we used our discretion to select the better
source.
Optical Character Recognition (OCR)
Unsurprisingly, the principal OCR challenge arises from the dense New Variorum footnotes. In these
footnotes, abbreviated citations, small type, human marginalia, italics, and an otherwise laconic
style create conditions substantially more difficult to scan than the main play text. The pipeline was
therefore optimized for the cases most consequential for scholarly use such as abbreviated citation
forms, italic lemma markers, small-type character confusions, and note boundaries. Common error
modes in the scans included punctuation ambiguity in abbreviated citations, italic/roman distinction
loss, and character substitutions such as “rn” for “m” in small type. These recurring patterns make
the corpus a strong test case for a correction pipeline that combines image-grounded OCR with
structured post-processing.
Processing Pipeline
There are three stages to the processing pipeline for www.newvariorum.com.
In stage one, raw UTF-8 text is read out of each page image to allow the pipeline to maintain some
level of spatial arrangement.
Stage two passes each page’s OCR output to Google Gemini together with the original page image
and volume-level bibliographic context, the model’s one-million-token context window allowing
parallel consultation of a volume’s entire bibliography alongside the current page. The stage is
multimodal. The model consults the scan when resolving ambiguous small-type footnotes, italic
lemma markers, and split citation blocks. The system prompt instructed the model to correct
character-level OCR errors, restore truncated abbreviations, re-join split citations, and preserve
the italic/roman distinction. Correction runs once per page; corrector passes are not chained over
corrected output, in line with the finding of Zhang et al. (2024) that iterated correction propagates

errors. Evidence that this stage repaired rather than rewrote—that it did not paraphrase, modernize,
or invent—rests not on prompt-level assurance but on the corpus-level fidelity verification reported
below.
Stage three parses the processed text into act/scene boundaries, line numbers, speaker attribution,
stage direction, and distinct commentary entry using regular expressions created specifically to
conform to the Variorum’s style.
Across these stages, many critic surname abbreviations are expanded into readable forms— Johns.
becomes JOHNSON, frequently with edition years—while collation-block abbreviations generally
retain their print forms. Expansion occurs at digitization time, not at display time; the platform
serves note text as stored, and per-play critic indexes supply full bibliographic identification.
Architecture: JSON over TEI
We examined TEI and developed a working prototype of TEI-encoded Macbeth, Act 1, before
deciding on JSON. Three factors contributed to this decision.
First, since the platform requires zero-dependency static hosting—that is, the application must
run without an XML database back-end—the choice of data structure was limited by storage
space requirements. The prototype TEI document for a well-annotated version of Macbeth was
approximately 50 megabytes and simply could not be loaded quickly enough into the browser.
Second, the platform requires fast O(1) lookups at the line level. For this purpose, we chose to
represent the annotation information in a flat JSON object so that JavaScript’s native property
access could be used.
Third, the platform integrates an artificial intelligence system, and this system expects data
represented as JSON. As such, we did not want to incur additional overhead associated with
serializing and deserializing TEI documents for every API call.
There is a standard objection to this choice. Prevailing practice in digital scholarly editing treats
TEI as the archival format of record, with JSON or HTML generated downstream as delivery formats
(Sahle, 2016); on that model, the platform could have maintained TEI masters and derived its JSON
corpus from them. For a project producing a new critical edition, that hybrid architecture is clearly
correct, as the TEI layer captures editorial decisions in an interchangeable, richly documented form.
But www.newvariorum.com introduces no new editorial content. The scholarly record it transmits
lives in the original page images and in the faithful transcription of Furness’s apparatus, both of
which are preserved and published; a TEI intermediary would document no editorial judgment
that the transcription does not already capture, while adding an encoding and maintenance layer
with real costs and no additional scholarly yield. This is a concrete instance of the paper’s central
distinction: infrastructure for accessing historical editions is a different undertaking from creating
critical editions, and the two warrant different architectures.
Editorial Alignment
The platform uses two lineation regimes, reflecting the current state of an ongoing migration.
The Folger Digital Texts TEI serves as the alignment spine for Othello : the alignment pipeline
(scripts/folger tei/align nv to folger.py) is a version-controlled, deterministic computational
task that ingests the Folger TEI source to build a linear spine of reference points—where “reference
point” refers to both the Folger act.scene.line anchors for dialogue lines and the SD * identifiers for
6
--- PAGE 7 ---
stage directions (SDs). Using Python’s SequenceMatcher with normalized text, each Variorum line
reference is matched against that spine, with per-play rule sets handling merged-scene edge cases.
Because the pipeline can be re-run against new or revised versions of the Folger TEI source files,
alignment errors are corrected by regeneration rather than by hand.
For the remaining plays, reading text and line keys derive from the New Variorum ingest pipeline
(OCR, structured intermediate documents, JSON), using each edition’s own lineation; commentary is
attached by scene and line number rather than by Folger anchors. The design rationale for Folger as
the alignment target—stable act-scene-line referencing, modernized spelling, open licensing—applies
corpus-wide, and extending the Othello -style alignment to the remaining plays is ongoing engineering
work rather than current production behavior.
To address the need for a uniform editorial procedure regarding stage directions, the frontend
classifies a line as a stage direction if and only if its string representation contains an opening and
closing square bracket. That classification affects how the line is processed downstream, determining
both how speakers’ names are displayed across all twenty-one plays and how the line itself is rendered.
The heuristic is deliberately simple and has known failure modes, since square brackets appear in
the Variorum apparatus for other purposes. An audit across all twenty-one playtext volumes (67,808
play lines) found 52 misclassified lines (0.08% of play lines; 2.31% of bracket-wrapped lines), chiefly
dialogue or note fragments accidentally bracketed during digitization; it turns out the failure mode
is, at least in this case, cosmetic: mis-set typography, and no note text is affected.
In the Othello alignment specifically, the pipeline assigned stable Folger anchors to all 3,763 act- and
scene-level line units in the spine (3,631 dialogue lines; 132 stage directions), and all 918 commentary
notes were placed on their 676 anchored lines, with zero placement failures in the alignment review
log. The eighteen spine entries not covered are Dramatis Personae items, which sit outside the Folger
act-scene-line structure. The principal difficulty during development was concentrated in stage
directions, where the task is determining whether an SD -referenced item belongs to the previous or
subsequent line; the per-play rule sets described above resolved these cases.
Verification of Textual Fidelity to the Printed New Variorum
The electronic corpus of the New Variorum website was evaluated against the Furness New Variorum
print editions to determine whether the notes presented on the site faithfully reproduce the scholarly
apparatus of the source volumes. The assessment addressed two distinct questions. First, are the
notes on the website complete (e.g., not truncated or missing material at their boundaries)? Second,
does the wording of the notes on the website correspond to the printed New Variorum edition,
allowing for the predictable noise introduced when nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century books
are digitized.
Bottom line. For scholarly use, the New Variorum website reproduces the printed New Variorum
faithfully. The note text on the website is not a paraphrase, nor is the text a synthetic rewrite of
Furness; the notes track the print tradition closely enough that a reader consulting the site alongside
the original volumes would encounter essentially the same editorial material in the overwhelming
majority of cases. Residual uncertainty is confined to a thin tail of edge cases—chiefly notes checked
against comparatively noisy witness scans—not to a systematic departure from the source editions.
Witnesses and verification design. Verification ground truth was drawn from transcriptions of
djvu.txt files provided by the Internet Archive, made from the same Furness volumes that served
as bibliographic sources for the edition (mapped per play in nv witness map.py). The witness layer

is entirely separate from the ingestion pipeline that produced the website’s JSON (page images
run through Tesseract/ABBYY and structured extraction), so convergence between the website’s
text and the witness text cannot be due to common tools employed during ingestion. Instead, it
indicates convergence on the printed record.
Three complementary checks were applied, each offering increasing depth and greater assurance,
and each employing different criteria.
Corpus-wide tail verification. Every note in the corpus (N = 23,094 across the 21 works)
was given a score based on identifying its last ninety characters within the corresponding witness
(rapidfuzz.fuzz.partial ratio ≥ 75 on normalized strings). Tail verification is sensitive to
truncation and splice errors at the ends of notes, which are exactly the errors it exists to catch,
while remaining robust to minor variations in OCR. By this method, 98.4% of all notes passed
(22,715/23,094; 95% Wilson interval 98.2–98.5%; Table 2). Of the works whose performance fell below
the average, Othello (93.9%) reflects a play file that mixes Furness apparatus with Folger-derived
gloss forms, consistent with the two-regime alignment described above.

Repair-cohort controls. Roughly eight hundred notes had earlier been repaired by copying their
missing endings from the witness itself, which raises an obvious worry: those endings will match
the witness by definition. To guard against circularity—that repaired notes might pass verification
because witness text was spliced into completions—the tail-verify pass rate was recomputed separately
for notes never appearing in the repair workbook (n = 22,270; 98.36%), the full repair cohort (n =
824; 98.30%), and spliced completions alone (n = 734; 98.91%). The repaired notes do not inflate
the headline figure; if anything, spliced completions perform marginally better than untouched notes.
In plain terms, the notes never touched by any repair passed at almost exactly the corpus-wide rate,
so the headline figure does not depend on the repairs at all.

Prior to reporting results, the measuring instrument itself needed to be corrected. The original
full-span protocol contained an identifiable instrument artifact. End-anchor over-capture generated
witness windows roughly twice the length of each note being evaluated, which lowered the similarity
scores ( fuzz.ratio) and produced false classifications of interior divergence even when nearly
perfect tail scores were achieved. The protocol was modified so that the tail-match position in the
witness, the same localization used in the corpus tail verification, defines the right boundary of the
span, with the start anchor defining the left. When tail localization failed, a conservative fallback
estimated span length at ±15% of normalized note length and flagged the comparison as estimated.
The score required to pass was held constant at 75 throughout the entire protocol, and no tuning
toward a target pass rate was performed. Only the location of the comparison window changed
under the corrected instrument, never the score required to pass.
Using the corrected instrument, 68.2% of anchored notes in the sample passed automated full-span
matching (122/179). Automated failures were not accepted at face value. All interior-divergence
cases (n = 55) were adjudicated by the author, who examined each case side by side, the electronic
note text against its bounded witness span, recording the play, line number, and witness identifier for
each. Every adjudicated case was classified as the result of witness OCR degradation—primarily two-
column apparatus interleaving in the scanned witness, long- s and ligature garble, and orthographic
normalization—not editorial rewriting or fabrication on the site. Two additional automated span-
mismatch cases, in which both the tail and full-span scores fell below threshold, were reviewed
separately and determined to be extraction-boundary artifacts in the witness window, not genuine
divergence from Furness. In every examined case the printed book agreed with the site; the failures
belonged to the scan used for checking, not to the corpus. Following adjudication, every sampled
note that could be tested proved faithful to print across its entire span, and no instance of genuine
interior divergence, paraphrase, or invention was found. The adjudicated cases were independently
reviewed by a second reader (see Acknowledgments).

Analysis Tier Structure
The platform provides users with three different levels of analysis: Basic (a short definition or gloss),
Expert (an extended essay, typically multi-paragraph), and Full Fathom Five (a variorum-style
annotation of approximately 800 to 1,200 words). Each level is serviced by a Netlify serverless
function routing to OpenAI’s API, although a parallel Deno Edge Function utilizes Anthropic’s
Claude agents as both a fallback and for purposes of A/B testing.
The platform’s AI tiers also use three public-domain works that provide lexical explanations for, and
biblical allusions behind, obscure words or phrases. For Key Words and Glosses, the system retrieves
entries from C. T. Onions’s A Shakespeare Glossary (1911; rev. ed. 1919). This standard lexicon,
compiled by an Oxford English Dictionary co-editor, focuses on obsolete words, idioms, and Early
Modern forms that may impede modern readers. Alexander Schmidt’s Shakespeare-Lexicon (3rd ed.,
1902), a classic lexicon offering broader coverage of the dramatic vocabulary, is retrieved in parallel
with Onions for the same passage lemmas; Onions remains the primary glossary in prompts and
citations, while Schmidt appears when it adds coverage or a more complete entry. Retrieved glossary
text is injected into the analysis prompt prior to the model’s invocation, ensuring that glosses are
traceable to named sources rather than undocumented model memory. For the Sources section, the
platform also draws, where appropriate, on the 1599 Geneva Bible, an English translation widely
familiar to Shakespeare’s Protestant contemporaries. Geneva matches are found by deterministic
phrase matching against the 1599 text, and the system presents them as possible parallels rather
than confirmed allusions unless the wording is exact.
To broaden the lexical scope beyond Shakespeare-specific reference works, we integrated materials
from the University of Toronto’s Lexicons of Early Modern English (LEME), whose transcriptions
are distributed publicly under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license. Plain-text exports from
LEME were indexed server-side and are deterministically queried when a user selects a passage for
analysis. The retrieved entries are then incorporated into the model prompt and displayed in the
interface exactly as transcribed in the server-side indices, alongside matches from Onions, Schmidt,
and the Geneva Bible; the retrieved text is reproduced without LLM paraphrase at retrieval time,
while the surrounding analysis prose remains model-generated and may paraphrase. The primary
objective of this integration is not to supplant existing Shakespeare glossaries but to contextualize
challenging or historically significant diction within the contemporary lexicographical record. This
approach aims to illuminate what constituted a “hard word” in Shakespeare’s era, how such words
were defined around that time, and the semantic fields a Jacobean reader might have recognized.

As with other lexical sources, the model does not directly query LEME. Instead, matches are
computed on the server, and only the supplied text is quoted or paraphrased in the analysis. This
design ensures the scholarly record remains inspectable, allowing a curious reader to compare the
generated commentary against the exact retrieved entry.
LEME primarily contributes lemmas attested in indexed hard-word lexicons or Florio/Cotgrave
English glosses. As a result, Shakespeare-specific coinages, proper names, and many noun senses
found in bilingual dictionaries may be absent. Nevertheless, LEME provides a contemporaneous
lexical stratum that Onions and Schmidt alone cannot offer, packaged in a citable and verifiable
form.
On the website, lexical entries are deterministically retrieved from server-side indices and displayed
as transcribed in those indices, not as re-keyed facsimiles of printed pages. Onions is primarily
ingested from an Internet Archive OCR transcription, merged with the Perseus Digital Library’s
professionally keyed text where available and more complete. Schmidt is ingested from Internet
Archive OCR, with regex corrections for systematic errors (e.g., “Yen.” for “Ven.,” the abbreviation
for Venus and Adonis ) and manual overrides for frequently retrieved lemmas, all applied at index-
build time. The Geneva Bible was ingested from eBible.org’s USFM edition of the 1599 text. For
its part, LEME materials use the project’s publicly distributed plain-text exports.
Of course, it is entirely possible that displayed entries may still inherit OCR or parsing artifacts.
As a prophylactic, we disclose the digital witness used, and we treat the retrieved-sources panel as
the citable record of what the system actually retrieved rather than as a guaranteed diplomatic
transcription of the original edition. While variorum notes remain the authoritative historical
apparatus where available, these additional sources provide essential lexical and scriptural grounding
that the experimental AI tiers would otherwise lack.
Full Fathom Five Prompt Requirements
The Full Fathom Five prompt requires ten specific sections of analysis in a particular order: Plain-
Language Paraphrase; Language and Rhetoric; Synopsis; Key Words and Glosses; Historical Context;
Sources; Literary Analysis; Critical Reception; Similar Phrases in Other Plays; and New Variorum
Analysis.
Two design decisions shaped this prompt. First, the prompt mandates inclusion of at least one critical
perspective per century from the eighteenth through the twenty-first, including a Marxist reading and
two to three additional interpretive traditions—New Historicism, psychoanalysis, formalism, queer
theory, biography, post-colonial theory, and ecocriticism—each characterized without attribution
to named critics. This is a deliberate editorial policy. While Shakespeare scholarship dominates
large language models’ training datasets, those representations are historically skewed. By requiring
perspectives spanning centuries, the model is pushed toward an assessment that balances historically

Constraining Model Output
Early testing with GPT-4o showed that unconstrained literary-critical generation could introduce
unsupported critic names and dates. Reducing the temperature to 0.3 improved output stability—
consistent with Zhang et al. (2024), who found temperature adjustment among the most effective
levers on historical-text tasks—and that experience motivated the stronger architectural solution
described above: prohibiting named attribution in model-generated critical sections unless the
relevant material is supplied by a retrieval layer. As part of the deployment strategy, the platform
dynamically assigns gpt-4o for the Full Fathom Five tier and gpt-4o-mini for all other tiers.
Additionally, both functions enforce a ninety-second timeout and provide structured error handling,
ensuring that slow API responses result in clean error messages rather than an indeterminate loading
state.

Deterministic Text Matching in the Retrieval Layer
Unlike semantic similarity retrieval methods that produce thematically similar but textually dissimilar
commentary, the platform employs deterministic text matching rather than vector embedding to
determine which commentary is relevant to which piece of text. For a scholarly platform, false
positives are far worse than false negatives. Thus, rather than receiving potentially misleading
commentary from adjacent lines, users who select incorrect lines receive no commentary.
Monolithic Single-File SPA Platform Design
The platform is designed as a monolithic single-file SPA (Single Page Application) instead of a
component-based framework. A single-file SPA has zero dependency issues resulting from deprecated
dependencies and therefore does not introduce a maintenance burden that cannot be sustained
for long-term preservation. Given that a monolithic HTML/CSS/JS file can be hosted statically
anywhere indefinitely, compared to a React application that requires a build step that will fail as
dependencies become outdated, this design choice maximizes preservation options. The single file
comprises the application code only; play-level JSON corpora are fetched on demand when a reader
selects a play, so the application shell remains small while the textual corpus loads incrementally.
In addition, because everything about the application is contained within a single file, a developer
can easily view the contents of the application’s architecture through a single “View Source.” In
keeping with its open, inspection-oriented goals, this design decision helps preserve transparency.
No-Cache Policy Across Three Layers
JSON files are served with strict no-cache directives. The frontend appends cache-breaking query
parameters to every JSON fetch, and the serverless function fetches play JSON with explicit no-cache
directives for every invocation. Although this results in suboptimal delivery speed, this three-layer
no-cache policy prioritizes scholarly accuracy over delivery speed. Corrections made to erroneous
annotations should be instantly observable to subsequent users accessing content containing those
corrections.
Code Repository Design
As previously stated, all code resides in a single Git repository. Upon pushing updates to Netlify’s
main branch, Netlify triggers automatic builds for all components. Additionally, API keys are stored
within Netlify’s environment variables system and are encrypted accordingly.

Corpus Coverage
The platform covers all twenty-one public-domain pre-1956 dramatic editions, spanning 1871 to
1940. The two remaining pre-1956 editions, Troilus and Cressida (1953) and Richard II (1955),
remain under copyright and are excluded.

Latin label describes. Even Johnson, generally skeptical of such conjectures, was overborne by the proposal
and pronounced sties “not only better, but more likely to be Shakespeare’s word.”

is currently implemented only for Othello ; the remaining plays use the New Variorum editions’ own

this is not a prerequisite for use of the website.
Fidelity verification. The fidelity of the New Variorum website should be measured against the
digitized print volumes, even if OCR defects set a practical ceiling on this automated agreement.

Evaluation scope. The post-OCR correction literature, as discussed above, would reasonably expect
a held-out character-error-rate benchmark for the Gemini correction stage, an ablation comparing
OCR-only and OCR-plus-LLM ingest, and measured over-correction rates on clean passages (Boros
et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). We, however, do not report these experiments, as stage-level
character metrics would measure transcript repair in isolation; they would not establish that 23,094
commentary notes reach readers without systematic tail loss or platform-side invention, which is
the claim the project’s verification protocol is designed to support. Following Levchenko (2025) and
Backer and Hyman (2025), we instead treat evaluation as a layered inquiry. Character metrics are
the right tool for comparing models, while agreement with independent print witnesses is the right
tool for assuring the corpus that readers actually see. We report the latter across the full corpus of
the New Variorum editions.
AI analysis tier reliability. The lexical glosses on the website, drawing on the Onions and Schmidt
lexica, and the plain-language summaries are the most mature components of the AI tiers. The Full
Fathom Five analytical tier is intentionally framed as an interpretive orientation rather than as a
replacement for scholarship. Its strongest reliability feature is architectural in that named citations
come from grounded retrieval layers, while the model-generated critical sections are prevented from
inventing bibliographic attributions.
Lexical witness quality. The Onions and Schmidt indices derive from OCR transcriptions supple-
mented by parser improvements, build-time corrections, and, for Onions, incremental integration
with keyed Perseus text. Further expansion of keyed coverage will continue to improve the already
useful lexical layer.

Sustainability. The platform incurs API costs for each Full Fathom Five request. Per-request
exposure is bounded by the tier structure—the smaller gpt-4o-mini serves all tiers below Full
Fathom Five—and by the ninety-second timeout, and the generated tiers can be disabled entirely
without affecting the verbatim historical corpus, which is served statically. The generated tiers are
also model-agnostic. Because analysis requests route through a thin serverless layer, the underlying
model can be exchanged—for a less expensive open-weight alternative or for a stronger frontier
model as economics and capabilities shift—without any change to the corpus or the retrieval
layers, a substitution the parallel Claude-based function already demonstrates in practice. Three
structural features mitigate long-term risk. First, the monolithic architecture requires no build
dependencies, and the static components will remain accessible even if AI API access is discontinued.
Second, the JSON corpus is fully portable to any static host. Third, the deterministic retrieval
of historical notes bypasses the LLM entirely for the portion of the platform with the greatest
scholarly reliability. In addition, the experimental tiers that rely on AI are enhancements to access,
not the scholarly foundation; the New Variorum notes remain available even if a reader forgoes the
large-language-model tiers entirely.
Future Directions
The fidelity verification reported above could be strengthened by re-OCRing the hard-case witness
(Othello ) against cleaner page scans—following the quality-triaged re-processing approach that
Zhang et al. (2024) found most effective, in which the poorest-quality material is routed to re-
recognition rather than to model correction—and by a full note-by-note coverage census against
print. Integration of the Texas A&M digital editions of the two overlapping plays would create a
complete scholarly record spanning from 1871 to the present. Evaluating the accuracy of the tier’s
characterizations of critical traditions against published scholarship would further test the design.
Informal feedback from three readers with English literature degrees indicated the platform’s lexical
glosses were useful and accurate; a structured user study would allow this finding to be assessed
more rigorously.
Conclusion
When Horace Howard Furness began the New Variorum Shakespeare in 1871, he sought to democra-
tize access to centuries of accumulated scholarship. A century and a half later, complete sets of his
editions remain scarce; the abbreviations he used to save space on the page now require specialized
knowledge; and the scholarship he gathered is ready for a new form of access commensurate with
its continuing value.
The platform described here ( https://newvariorum.com ) makes twenty-one of those volumes
findable by line number in under a second. The historical commentary is delivered verbatim from
the corpus, never passing through the model. The AI features layered on top serve a different
purpose: helping a non-specialist begin to interpret 150 years of compressed critical argument about
a single line of Shakespeare. The lexical glosses, grounded in Onions’s glossary and Schmidt’s
lexicon, are traceable to their sources by design. The Full Fathom Five analytical tier, with its
century-spanning perspective requirement and provision for diverse critical traditions, attempts
something more ambitious, namely a historically balanced critical orientation rather than a rehearsal
21
--- PAGE 22 ---
of whatever critics happen to dominate the model’s training distribution. This is a principled design
goal, even as we acknowledge that its characterizations of those traditions are model-generated
summaries. Most fundamentally, we have attempted to create infrastructure that expands rather
than replaces human engagement with literary texts. The goal is not to automate scholarship but to
provide tools that enable more people to participate in the ongoing conversation about Shakespeare
that Furness began over 150 years ago.
